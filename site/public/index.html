Working out what someone is looking at from the electricity in their brain

Two people had a grid of electrodes placed straight onto the back of their brain before surgery. While the grid was in, they watched patterns flash on a screen, half a second at a time. I tried to work out which pattern was on screen using nothing but the voltage.

01 · What I set out to do

The plan I started with, and where it broke

The idea I copied is popular and goes like this. Run the pictures through an image model to turn each one into a list of numbers. Run the brain signal through a brain model to turn that into a list of numbers too. Then learn a map from one list to the other. Once you have that map, new brain data goes in and a picture comes out.

Two things went wrong straight away, and both turned out to be more interesting than the original plan.

Problem one

The pictures are not videos or photographs. They are stripes and grainy textures. Seven kinds, thirty versions of each. There is no photo to rebuild, so "reconstruct the image" had to mean something else.

Problem two

Every ready-made brain model I could find was trained on EEG, recorded from the scalp. Those models only keep frequencies up to about 40 or 125 Hz. In this kind of recording the useful signal sits above that.

02 · The main result

How much a model can see depends on how much of the signal it kept

I measured how much of the brain's reaction to a picture happens at each frequency. In the better of the two patients, only about a tenth of it sits below 40 Hz. That is the cutoff the SignalJEPA model was trained under. Everything above is thrown away before that model gets to look.

So I ran four ways of turning brain signal into numbers against each other, on the same trials, scored the same way. They line up in order of how much of the signal each one can still see.

The last row is the one I did not expect. I trained a small model on the raw recordings themselves, with the frequencies left in. It never saw a single label saying which picture was on screen. It beat the feature set built by hand from the published paper on this exact signal.

03 · Before any of that

First I checked the signal was really there

There is a known effect in this data. Stripe patterns set off a bump in the power spectrum somewhere between 30 and 80 Hz. Grainy textures do not, even though they push the brain just as hard overall. If my code could not find that, nothing after it would be worth reading. So I made it a test that has to pass before anything else runs.

It is there. The stripes make the bump, the textures do not, and the bump slides up in frequency as the stripes get finer. That last part I never tuned for. Getting it out the other end is a good sign the filtering is right.

05 · The honest part

What it cannot do

Telling the seven kinds of pattern apart works well. Telling apart two pictures of the same kind does not work at all, and I can say exactly why.

Inside one kind, the thirty versions differ in one thing only: where the stripes sit, the phase. Everything else is held fixed. So picking the right one out of thirty comes down entirely to whether phase can be read off the surface of the brain. It can, a little. The link is real and holds up under a shuffle test, but it is weak, and weak is not enough to separate thirty near-identical pictures.

There is a second reason, and it is a ceiling rather than a failure of the method. One patient saw every picture twice. I checked how much the brain's answer to the same picture repeats between the two viewings. For single trials it repeats at a correlation of about 0.22. No decoder can beat the reliability of the thing it is decoding.
