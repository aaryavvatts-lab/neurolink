Every figure here comes with a shuffle test and a range. The shuffle test asks how often a score this good turns up when the answers are scrambled. If a number has no test beside it, do not trust it.

01 · Four ways of reading the signal

Telling the seven kinds apart

Same trials, same split, same scoring. The only thing that changes is how the brain signal was turned into numbers before a plain linear model saw it.

02 · Where it goes wrong

The mistakes are the ones a visual system would make

Almost every error lands on a neighbour. A pattern gets read as a slightly finer or slightly coarser version of itself, or one grainy texture gets read as another. Very few errors cross between stripes and textures, which are the two groups the brain treats most differently.

03 · The result I did not expect

A model that has never seen your brain still works on it

The training used no labels at all. It read the raw recordings and tried to fill in pieces it had been shown with gaps punched in them. It never saw an event file and never learned which picture was on screen at any moment.

04 · Reading numbers off the signal

What can be read directly

Instead of trying to name the picture, this asks for its properties. How fine is the detail, how fast does the texture fall off, how much contrast is there. Each one is scored against pictures the model never saw.

05 · A test the image model failed

Does a modern vision model add anything here?

DINOv2 is a large vision model trained on 142 million photographs. I compared how it arranges these pictures against how the brain arranges them, and then asked whether it still matched once I removed everything a simple 1960s model of visual cortex already explains.

06 · Two ways to join the spaces

Ridge against the contrastive method

The approach I copied uses a contrastive setup, the kind behind CLIP: push each trial's brain code towards its own picture's code and away from everyone else's. I expected the plain linear method to win at this sample size. It did not. They come out level.

Worth saying they are not scored on quite the same footing. Ridge has to hit DINOv2's own space. The contrastive model is measured inside a space it was trained to make matching easy in. Both are tested on pictures neither had seen, so neither is cheating, but the comparison flatters the second one a little.

07 · The ceiling

What this cannot do, and why

Telling the seven kinds apart works. Picking the right one of thirty pictures of the same kind does not. Here is the evidence, in the order I found it.

Everything I would want a reader to hold against this

  • Two patients. One of them gives much weaker results than the other, and I show them separately rather than averaging the difference away.
  • The pictures are stripes and grainy textures. Nothing here shows any of it would work on photographs or faces, and the tool on this site warns you when your picture sits outside the range it learned.
  • The reconstruction is not general. It reads a handful of numbers off the signal and redraws a picture from them. It cannot draw something it was not told how to draw.
  • The two ready-made EEG models are being used outside what they were built for. Different recording method, different speed, and a half second response where one of them needs at least a second and a half of signal. Their poor showing here says they do not fit this problem. It does not say they are bad at what they were made for.
  • Patient 2's grid lost 35 of 96 contacts to the clinical bad list before I touched anything.
