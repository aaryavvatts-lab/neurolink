Methods

How this was actually done

Enough detail to argue with. All of it runs from one command, and the whole thing is on GitHub.

01

Cleaning the recording

Mains hum at 60 Hz and its multiples are filtered out. Contacts are dropped if the people who collected the data marked them bad, or if my own check flags them. My check looks at four things: whether a contact is flat, whether its variance is far from the rest of the grid, whether it is dominated by mains hum, and whether it has spikes far larger than anything else on the array.

That last one I got wrong the first time. I used a fixed cutoff on the largest spike, and it threw away 33 of 118 contacts. Over four minutes of brain recording a peak twelve to eighteen times the typical size is completely ordinary, so the cutoff was flagging normal data. Scoring each contact against the rest of its own grid instead brought it down to 8, and 7 of those were the same ones the data collectors had already marked.

What is left is re-referenced against the average of all the good contacts, then cut into windows from 200 ms before each picture to 700 ms after.

02

Splitting the spectrum in two

There are two different things happening in the same frequency range and they need separating. One is a real oscillation, a bump around 30 to 80 Hz that stripes set off. The other is a broad lift across all high frequencies that goes up whenever the brain is busy. If you just measure total power in the gamma range you mix them, and a decoder handed that one number cannot tell a stripe pattern from a grainy one.

So each trial's spectrum is fitted with two parts added together: a straight line in log space, which is the broad lift, plus a bump. The slope of the line is fixed for each contact using that contact's own blank-screen recordings, which lets the two parts move independently from trial to trial. The bump is only allowed to sit between 30 and 80 Hz. This follows the model in Hermes and colleagues' work on the same signal.

One practical note. Given where the bump sits and how wide it is, the rest of the fit is a straight line problem with an exact answer. So instead of running a slow search for every one of the 23,000 spectra, I sweep a grid over the bump's position and width and solve the rest in closed form for all spectra at once. Same answer, roughly a hundred times faster, and it cannot get stuck in the wrong place the way a search can.

03

The model I trained with no labels

This is a transformer with about 4.8 million numbers in it. It reads the recording as a picture of frequency against time, has patches of that picture blanked out, and has to fill them back in. It never sees which stimulus was on screen. It never opens the event file.

The blanking is done in blocks, not scattered dots. Neighbouring pieces of a spectrogram look almost the same, so scattered dots can be filled in by copying a neighbour and the model learns nothing. Whole strips of time and whole bands of frequency force it to use longer range structure.

The important choice is the sample rate. It runs at 512 samples a second, which keeps everything up to 200 Hz. That is the whole point. The ready-made models throw that range away before they start.

It gets 4.4 percent better at filling in gaps than simply guessing the average, which sounds small and is. Brain recordings are mostly noise and most of what is missing genuinely cannot be recovered. The number that matters is not the filling-in score, it is whether what it learned is useful afterwards, and it is.

04

Keeping the scoring honest

Splits are made by picture, not by trial. Every picture is either wholly in the training part or wholly in the test part, and each split checks this itself and stops the run if it finds an overlap. Scaling and dimension reduction are fitted inside the training part only, never on everything at once.

Every headline number gets a shuffle test. I scramble which answer belongs to which trial a thousand times and see how often a score that good turns up by accident. Ranges come from resampling the trials. Where a score has a level you would get by luck, that level is printed next to it rather than left for you to work out.

Retrieval is reported twice, once against all 210 pictures and once against only the thirty of the same kind. The first number alone cannot tell apart "found the picture" from "found the category", and on this data it is almost entirely the second.

05

Three bugs worth naming

Each of these produced confident, wrong numbers before I caught it. I am listing them because a methods section that reports no mistakes is not describing real work.

A filter that multiplied instead of filtering

To feed the ready-made EEG models I first had to bandpass the signal the way they expect. I built the filter in the obvious form, and at these sample rates the low edge sits at a normalised frequency near two ten-thousandths, where that form falls apart numerically. Instead of filtering, it multiplied the signal by about ten to the power of 105. Rebuilding it in second order sections fixed it. Left alone it would have fed those models garbage and made the main comparison on this site meaningless.

A model that ignored its own input

The SignalJEPA checkpoint I used ships without the part that encodes electrode position, which is exactly what its name says. So that part starts random, and it gets added after the trained layers. On this data the full model's output changed by four hundredths of a thousandth of its own size from trial to trial, and did not change at all when I scaled the input by fifty. It was reporting the random part, not the signal. Reading out from the trained layers directly fixed it.

A ceiling that gave a perfect score to a model that did nothing

I measured how repeatable each encoder was across two viewings of the same picture, and SignalJEPA came out at exactly 1.000. That should have been impossible. The measure was comparing patterns without first removing what every picture had in common, so a model that returned nearly the same answer no matter what it saw scored perfectly. Centring the measure fixed it and dropped the score to near zero, which is the honest answer.

06

The tools on this site

Both tools run entirely in your browser. Nothing is uploaded and there is no server doing the work.

The response tool measures your picture with a Fourier transform on a 512 by 512 crop, matching what the Python pipeline does, then applies a linear model whose weights are in a small file this page downloads. I checked the browser's measurements against the Python ones on one picture of each kind: contrast matches exactly, detail matches exactly, and the falloff number is within 0.24 at worst.

The decoder tool ships the real fitted weights. To make sure the browser version is not an approximation, I ran it over all 210 trials and compared against what the Python model says. Both give 0.8952. Same answer to four decimal places.
