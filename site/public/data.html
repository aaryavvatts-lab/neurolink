The recordings

Where this data came from

Two people waiting for epilepsy surgery had a grid of electrodes placed on the surface of their brain so doctors could find where their seizures start. While the grid was in, they agreed to watch some patterns on a screen.

01 · The task

What the patients did

A picture appeared for half a second, then the screen went blank grey for half a second, then the next picture. That repeated 420 times per run. There was a small dot in the middle of the screen that changed colour at random moments, and the job was to keep looking at the dot. One patient pressed a button when it changed. The other did not, because pressing the button made them look away.

The pictures filled about 25 degrees of the visual field, which is roughly what a dinner plate covers at arm's length.

Patient 2's grid had 35 of its 96 contacts marked bad by the people who collected the data, mostly because they sat over the part of the brain the seizures came from. That leaves 61 usable contacts against patient 1's 110, and it shows in every result on this site. I report the two separately rather than pooling them.

02 · The pictures

Seven kinds, thirty of each

Four of the seven are stripes, getting finer each time. The other three are grainy textures, getting smoother each time. I did not take the labels on trust. I measured every picture myself, straight from the pixels, and the numbers came out matching what the dataset says they should be.

03 · What caught me out

Three things about this data I got wrong at first

The bad channel list is real, and I nearly ignored it

I first looked at only the first few columns of the channel file and concluded nothing was marked bad. There is a status column further along, and it marks 7 contacts in one patient and 35 in the other. Before I noticed, I had written my own detector that finds broken contacts from the signal itself. When I finally compared the two lists, my detector had found exactly the same 7 contacts in patient 1, plus one more. That was a good sign for the detector, and a lesson about reading the whole file.

The two patients name their electrodes differently

In patient 1 the recording calls a contact "1" while the position file calls the same contact "iEEG1". In patient 2 both files say "iEEG1". If you join the two files on the name, patient 1 silently comes out with no positions at all. Everything has to be reduced to the number at the end first.

All the stripes point the same way

This one changed the whole project. I assumed the thirty versions of each stripe pattern would differ in angle. They do not. Every stripe picture in the set is at the same angle, to within a rounding error. What changes between the thirty is where the stripes sit, the phase, and nothing else.

That means telling two pictures of the same kind apart is not a question of whether the brain signal carries angle or fineness. It is only a question of whether it carries phase. That single fact explains the main limit of this whole project, and I would have missed it if I had trusted the labels instead of measuring.

04 · Getting it yourself

The data is public

It sits on OpenNeuro as dataset ds005953, released under CC0, which means anyone can use it for anything without asking. The code on this site fetches it with a one line script. I did not collect any of it and I have no connection to the people who did.

The patients gave permission for their recordings to be shared. Nothing on this site identifies anyone, and the brain surfaces shown in the viewer are stripped of the facial features that would make a scan identifiable.
